                     STATEMENT OF JURISDICTION

     The district court had subject-matter jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C.
Section 1331 because Plaintiff pleaded a claim arising under federal law, and
under 28 U.S.C. Section 1332(d) because the amount in controversy exceeds
$5,000,000, the proposed class exceeds 100 members, and minimal diversity is
satisfied. This Court has appellate jurisdiction over the class-certification
order under Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(f) and 28 U.S.C. Section 1291. A petition for
permission to appeal was timely filed within 14 days of the certification
order, and a motions panel of this Court granted permission on February 3,
2026. The order under review disposes of the certification question and the
denial of Nordhaven's motion to dismiss for lack of standing, which the panel
granted leave to review as inextricably intertwined with certification.

     The certification order was entered on December 18, 2025. (App. 1.) The
notice of appeal followed the grant of the Rule 23(f) petition. This appeal is
therefore properly before the Court.


                    STATEMENT OF THE ISSUES

     1. Whether a plaintiff who alleges only that a company retained a data
record longer than a state statute permits, without alleging that anyone ever
saw the record or that it caused her any concrete harm, has suffered an injury
in fact sufficient for Article III standing under Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins and
TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez.

     2. Whether the district court abused its discretion in certifying a
class under Rule 23(b)(3) where liability turns on whether each member's
record was disclosed and relied upon, and where the plaintiff's damages model
measures a harm the liability theory does not establish.

     3. Whether the district court erred in construing the Consumer Data
Protection Act to impose liability without a knowing violation, and whether
that construction is preempted to the extent it conflicts with the Fair Credit
Reporting Act.

                    STATEMENT OF THE CASE

     This is an interlocutory appeal from an order certifying a nationwide
class of roughly 1.4 million consumers and denying a motion to dismiss for
lack of standing. The suit rests on a single premise: that Nordhaven
Analytics, Inc. ("Nordhaven") kept certain audit records in its Verified
Ledger system for longer than the retention window set by the Consumer Data
Protection Act ("CDPA"). Plaintiff Marisol Calloway does not allege that any
record about her was ever disclosed to a third party, that any decision was
ever made about her on the basis of such a record, or that she suffered any
out-of-pocket loss. She alleges only that the records existed, and existed too
long. On that theory, she seeks statutory damages for herself and for
1.4 million people she has never met and about whom she knows nothing. [1]

     Nordhaven moved to dismiss for lack of Article III standing and, in the
alternative, for failure to state a claim. The district court denied the
motion. It held that the CDPA's retention limit creates a substantive right,
that a violation of that right is itself a concrete injury, and that the risk
that an over-retained record "could" be disclosed supplies concreteness even
where no disclosure occurred. (App. 22-31.) The court then certified a
Rule 23(b)(3) class, reasoning that whether Nordhaven over-retained records is
a common question capable of classwide proof, and that individualized questions
of disclosure and reliance go to damages, not liability. (App. 40-58.)

     Both rulings are wrong, and each independently requires reversal. The
Supreme Court held in TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), that
"[o]nly those plaintiffs who have been concretely harmed by a defendant's
statutory violation may sue that private defendant over the violation in
federal court." Id. at 427. A record that sits unseen in a database harms no
one. And a class cannot be certified under Rule 23(b)(3) when the very
elements of liability -- disclosure, causation, and reliance -- must be proved
person by person. The certification order collapses that inquiry by relabeling
liability questions as damages questions, contrary to Comcast Corp. v. Behrend,
569 U.S. 27 (2013), and Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338 (2011).

     The order should be reversed, the class decertified, and the case
remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims of any plaintiff who cannot
show concrete harm.

                       STATEMENT OF FACTS

A.  Nordhaven's data-integrity business

     Nordhaven Analytics is a data-integrity company. It does not sell
consumer profiles, and it does not make lending, employment, or insurance
decisions. Its business is narrow: it provides enterprise customers -- banks,
hospital systems, and logistics firms -- with a tamper-evident audit trail for
the records those customers already hold. (App. 118.) When a customer's own
system writes a record, Nordhaven's software computes a cryptographic digest of
the record and stores that digest, together with a timestamp, in an
append-only log called the Verified Ledger. (App. 119-120.) The purpose is to
let the customer later prove that a record has not been altered.

     The record that matters here is not the underlying consumer data, which
never leaves the customer's control. It is the audit entry: a digest, a
timestamp, and a reference identifier. (App. 121.) An audit entry cannot be
run backward to reconstruct the consumer record it describes; it is, by
design, a one-way fingerprint. [2] Nordhaven's engineers testified without
contradiction that no human being at Nordhaven ever reads the entries, that
they are never transmitted to anyone other than the customer that created
them, and that they are used only when a customer runs an integrity check.
(App. 122-124.)

     Nordhaven serves 380 enterprise customers. Across those customers, the
Verified Ledger holds approximately 2.1 billion audit entries. (App. 118.) The
entries are retained according to schedules the customers set, because the
customers -- not Nordhaven -- decide how long they must keep their own audit
trails to satisfy their own regulators. (App. 126.)

B.  The Verified Ledger product and its audit records

     The CDPA, enacted in 2023, imposes a default retention limit on
"covered records." It provides that a "data intermediary shall not retain a
covered record for longer than twenty-four months after the record's last use,
unless a longer period is required by law or authorized by the consumer." Cal.
Civ. Code Section 1799.14 (fictional). The statute defines a "covered record"
as "information that identifies or is linked to a particular consumer." Id.
Section 1799.11. It creates a private right of action and provides for
statutory damages of "not less than $100 and not more than $750 per consumer
per incident," or actual damages, whichever is greater. Id. Section 1799.31.

     Nordhaven built retention controls into the Verified Ledger. Each audit
entry carries a "last use" timestamp, and a nightly job purges entries whose
last use exceeds the applicable schedule. (App. 129.) The dispute in this case
arises from a defect in that job. For a period of about eleven weeks in early
2024, a configuration error caused the purge job to skip entries belonging to
a subset of customers whose schedules had been migrated to a new format.
(App. 131-133.) During those eleven weeks, roughly 1.4 million audit entries
that should have been purged remained in the ledger. When Nordhaven discovered
the error, it corrected the configuration, purged the affected entries, and
notified the affected customers. (App. 134.) No entry was disclosed to anyone
outside the customer that created it, and no customer reported any downstream
consequence. (App. 135.)

     The plaintiff's theory treats every one of those 1.4 million entries as a
separate CDPA violation, and seeks the statutory minimum of $100 for each --
roughly $140 million -- without regard to whether any consumer was ever
affected in any way. [3]

C.  The named plaintiff and the disputed record

     Marisol Calloway is a customer of Brightline Credit Union, one of
Nordhaven's enterprise customers. (App. 141.) In 2021, Ms. Calloway applied
for and received an auto loan from Brightline. As part of servicing that loan,
Brightline wrote records to its own systems, and Nordhaven's software computed
audit entries for some of those records. (App. 142.) One of Ms. Calloway's
audit entries was among the 1.4 million that the purge defect failed to remove
on schedule; it remained in the ledger for approximately nine weeks longer
than the twenty-four-month window allowed. (App. 144.)

     Ms. Calloway learned of the entry only when she received Brightline's
notice. (App. 146.) She does not contend that the entry was ever disclosed to
anyone, that anyone ever read it, that it was used to make any decision about
her, or that it affected her credit, her finances, or anything else. (App.
147-149.) Asked at her deposition whether she could identify any way in which
the over-retained entry had harmed her, she answered: "I just don't like that
they kept it." (App. 151.) She testified that she had not lost sleep, had not
sought any professional help, had not changed any financial arrangement, and
had incurred no expense. (App. 151-153.)

     Nordhaven does not minimize the retention error; it corrected it and
disclosed it. But the question in this Court is not whether Nordhaven made a
mistake. It is whether a person who cannot identify any effect of that mistake
on her life may nonetheless invoke the power of a federal court, and may drag
1.4 million absent class members -- about whom even less is known -- along with
her.

D.  Proceedings below

     Nordhaven moved under Rule 12(b)(1) to dismiss for lack of standing and
under Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim. (App. 12.) The district
court denied both motions. On standing, the court reasoned that the CDPA's
retention limit is a "substantive protection," that its violation is a
"concrete injury in the eyes of the law," and that the "material risk" of
future disclosure supplied concreteness independent of any actual disclosure.
(App. 22-31.) On the merits, the court held that the CDPA imposes liability for
over-retention regardless of the intermediary's state of mind, reading the
statute to require no showing that the violation was knowing. (App. 33-38.)

     Plaintiff then moved to certify a nationwide Rule 23(b)(3) class of "all
persons whose covered records were retained by Nordhaven beyond the period
permitted by the CDPA." (App. 41.) The district court certified the class. It
found that the "central question" -- whether Nordhaven over-retained records --
was common and provable with Nordhaven's own logs, and it treated disclosure,
causation, and reliance as issues going only to the measure of damages. (App.
46-53.) On damages, the court accepted the plaintiff's expert's proposal to
award the statutory minimum per entry, holding that this "arithmetic" model
satisfied Comcast. (App. 54-57.) The court certified the class and this appeal
followed.

                    SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT

     I. The class should never have reached certification because the named
plaintiff lacks Article III standing, and so do the vast majority of the
absent members. Standing requires an injury that is "concrete and
particularized," Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992), and
concreteness does not follow automatically from a statutory violation. In
Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330 (2016), the Supreme Court held that a
plaintiff does not "automatically satisf[y] the injury-in-fact requirement
whenever a statute grants a person a statutory right and purports to authorize
that person to sue to vindicate that right." Id. at 341. TransUnion made the
point unmistakable: "No concrete harm, no standing." 594 U.S. at 442.

     Ms. Calloway alleges no concrete harm. She does not allege disclosure,
misuse, or any decision made about her. Her injury is that a fingerprint of a
record she already knew about sat in a database nine weeks too long, unseen.
That is the paradigmatic "bare procedural violation, divorced from any
concrete harm," that Spokeo holds insufficient. Id. at 341. The district
court's two theories -- that the violation is itself the injury, and that the
"risk" of disclosure supplies concreteness -- were both foreclosed by
TransUnion, which rejected risk-of-future-harm as a basis for standing in a
suit for damages, 594 U.S. at 436-38, and which reaffirmed that Congress (much
less a state legislature) cannot "enact an injury into existence," id. at 426
(quotation marks omitted).

     II. Even if some class members had standing, the class could not be
certified, because individual questions predominate over common ones.
Rule 23(b)(3) demands that common questions "predominate over any questions
affecting only individual members," and the Supreme Court has insisted on a
"rigorous analysis" that often "overlap[s] with the merits." Comcast, 569 U.S.
at 33-34; Wal-Mart, 564 U.S. at 351. Here, once standing is taken seriously,
the questions that decide liability -- was a given member's record disclosed,
to whom, with what effect -- are irreducibly individual. The district court
avoided that conclusion only by relabeling those liability questions as
damages questions. That relabeling cannot survive Comcast, which holds that a
damages model must "measure only those damages attributable to" the plaintiff's
theory of liability, 569 U.S. at 35, and Wal-Mart, which forbids certification
that would decide liability by "Trial by Formula," 564 U.S. at 367.

     The plaintiff's damages model compounds the error. It awards the same
$100 to every class member regardless of whether that member suffered anything,
which means it does not measure harm at all; it measures the number of entries.
A model unmoored from the injury cannot establish predominance. Comcast, 569
U.S. at 35; Olean Wholesale Grocery Cooperative, Inc. v. Bumble Bee Foods LLC,
31 F.4th 651, 665-69 (9th Cir. 2022) (en banc).

     III. Finally, the district court misread the CDPA. The statute's operative
verb -- "shall not retain" -- appears in a section whose enforcement provision
speaks of a "violation," and the surrounding structure, the statutory damages
range keyed to culpability, and the ordinary presumption against strict
liability all show that the legislature required a knowing violation. The
district court's contrary construction, imposing liability for an inadvertent
configuration error, is not compelled by the text and produces absurd results.
And to the extent the CDPA is read to impose strict, classwide liability on a
data intermediary for retaining audit fingerprints that duplicate records the
customer keeps under federal law, it conflicts with and is preempted by the
Fair Credit Reporting Act's calibrated scheme. Geier v. American Honda Motor
Co., 529 U.S. 861, 873-74 (2000); Wyeth v. Levine, 555 U.S. 555, 563-65 (2009).

     Each of these three grounds independently requires reversal.

                     STANDARD OF REVIEW

     Standing is a question of law reviewed de novo. TransUnion, 594 U.S. at
430-31. Because standing is jurisdictional, the plaintiff bears the burden of
establishing it "with the manner and degree of evidence required at the
successive stages of the litigation," Lujan, 504 U.S. at 561, and "for each
claim [s]he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought,"
DaimlerChrysler Corp. v. Cuno, 547 U.S. 332, 352 (2006).

     A class-certification order is reviewed for abuse of discretion, but a
district court "necessarily abuse[s] its discretion if it based its ruling on
an erroneous view of the law." Comcast, 569 U.S. at 33 n.4 (quotation marks
omitted). Whether the district court applied the correct legal standard under
Rule 23 is reviewed de novo. Olean, 31 F.4th at 663. Questions of statutory
interpretation and preemption are reviewed de novo. Wyeth, 555 U.S. at 563.


                                ARGUMENT

I.   PLAINTIFF LACKS ARTICLE III STANDING BECAUSE A BARE STATUTORY VIOLATION,
     WITHOUT CONCRETE HARM, IS NOT AN INJURY IN FACT.

     The judicial power of the United States extends only to "Cases" and
"Controversies." U.S. Const. art. III, Section 2. That limitation is enforced
through the doctrine of standing, whose "irreducible constitutional minimum"
requires an injury in fact that is "concrete and particularized" and "actual
or imminent," fairly traceable to the defendant, and redressable. Lujan, 504
U.S. at 560-61. This case fails at the first and most basic requirement.
Because the district court's standing ruling rests on legal errors that
TransUnion squarely forecloses, review is de novo and reversal follows.

A.   Article III requires a concrete injury, and a statutory violation does
     not supply one automatically.

     The Supreme Court has now twice held, in Spokeo and TransUnion, that a
plaintiff cannot manufacture standing simply by identifying a statutory
violation. In Spokeo, a consumer sued under the Fair Credit Reporting Act,
alleging that a "people search" website had published inaccurate information
about him. 578 U.S. at 336. The Ninth Circuit had found standing because the
statutory right was particular to him. The Supreme Court vacated, holding that
particularization is "necessary" but "not sufficient": a plaintiff must also
show that the injury is "concrete" -- meaning "de facto," "real," and not
"abstract." Id. at 340. Crucially, the Court held that "Article III standing
requires a concrete injury even in the context of a statutory violation," and
that a plaintiff does not "automatically satisf[y] the injury-in-fact
requirement whenever a statute grants a person a statutory right and purports
to authorize that person to sue." Id. at 341. A "bare procedural violation,
divorced from any concrete harm," will not do. Id.

     TransUnion removed any remaining doubt. There, a class of 8,185 consumers
sued a credit-reporting agency that had flagged their files as potential
matches to a government terrorist watchlist. 594 U.S. at 419. For 1,853 class
members, the misleading reports had been disclosed to third parties; for the
remaining 6,332, the reports sat in TransUnion's internal files but were never
disclosed to anyone. Id. at 431-32. The Court held that the first group had
standing and the second did not. The difference was concrete harm: the
disclosed reports worked a harm with a "close relationship" to the common-law
tort of defamation, while the undisclosed reports harmed no one. Id. at 432-34.
"The mere presence of an inaccuracy in an internal credit file, if it is not
disclosed to a third party, causes no concrete harm." Id. at 434. The Court's
now-canonical formulation bears repeating: "No concrete harm, no standing." Id.
at 442. [4]

     The parallel to this case is exact, and it is fatal to the plaintiff. Like
the undisclosed credit files in TransUnion, the over-retained audit entries
here "sat in [Nordhaven's] internal files." Ms. Calloway's entry was never
disclosed, never read, never used. If the undisclosed files in TransUnion --
which falsely branded consumers as potential terrorists -- caused "no concrete
harm," then a correct, unread cryptographic fingerprint that lingered nine
weeks too long cannot possibly clear the bar. The district court's holding that
the statutory violation is "itself" the injury is precisely the reasoning
Spokeo and TransUnion reject.

     The district court leaned on the proposition that legislatures may
"elevat[e]" previously inadequate harms to the status of legally cognizable
injuries. See Spokeo, 578 U.S. at 341. But that principle has limits the court
ignored. A legislature's judgment is "instructive and important," but it is not
dispositive, because "Article III standing requires a concrete injury even in
the context of a statutory violation." Id. TransUnion made the limit explicit:
"Congress may enact legal prohibitions and obligations. But under Article III,
an injury in law is not an injury in fact. And only those plaintiffs who have
been concretely harmed by a defendant's statutory violation may sue." 594 U.S.
at 427. A state legislature has no greater power than Congress to "enact an
injury into existence." Id. at 426. [5]

B.   The asserted informational and risk-based injuries are not concrete under
     TransUnion.

     Recognizing that the naked violation cannot support standing, the
district court offered two fallback theories. Neither survives TransUnion.

     1. The "risk of disclosure" theory. The district court held that the
"material risk" that an over-retained entry "could" someday be disclosed
supplied concreteness. (App. 29.) TransUnion forecloses this reasoning in a
suit for damages. The Court there addressed exactly the argument that exposure
to a risk of future harm is a present concrete injury, and it held that "the
mere risk of future harm, standing alone, cannot qualify as a concrete harm --
at least unless the exposure to the risk of future harm itself causes a
separate concrete harm." 594 U.S. at 436-37. In a damages action, "the risk of
future harm on its own does not support Article III standing." Id. at 438. The
plaintiff seeks damages, not an injunction; the risk theory is therefore
unavailable as a matter of law. And in all events, the risk here is not
"material" or "imminent"; it is speculative in the extreme, resting on a chain
of hypothetical events -- a breach, an exfiltration, a reversal of a one-way
digest -- that the record affirmatively negates. See Clapper v. Amnesty
International USA, 568 U.S. 398, 409-14 (2013) (rejecting standing premised on a
"speculative chain of possibilities").

     2. The "informational injury" theory. The district court also suggested
that Ms. Calloway suffered an "informational injury" because the CDPA entitled
her to have the record deleted. (App. 30.) But TransUnion and Spokeo permit an
informational-injury theory only where the plaintiff was denied information to
which she was entitled and suffered "downstream consequences" from lacking it.
TransUnion, 594 U.S. at 441-42. Ms. Calloway was denied no information; she
received the very notice that told her the entry existed. She points to no
"downstream consequence" of any kind. The informational-injury cases are the
opposite of this one: they involve a plaintiff kept in the dark, not a
plaintiff complaining that a record about her was kept too long in a vault she
never had to open. [6]

     The absence of any common-law analogue confirms the point. TransUnion
instructs courts to ask whether the alleged injury bears a "close relationship
to a harm traditionally recognized as providing a basis for a lawsuit in
American courts." Id. at 424. Over-retention of an unseen record has no such
analogue. It is not defamation, because nothing was published. Restatement
(Second) of Torts Section 559 (1977). It is not intrusion upon seclusion,
because nothing was intruded upon. It is not conversion, breach of confidence,
or any recognized dignitary tort. The plaintiff's inability to name a
traditional harm is not a technicality; it is a signal that no concrete injury
exists. [7]

C.   Standing must be proved for every class member, not just the named
     plaintiff.

     The standing defect is not confined to Ms. Calloway. TransUnion holds that
"[e]very class member must have Article III standing in order to recover
individual damages." 594 U.S. at 431. A class action "does not relax the
requirement to establish standing to obtain relief," and the courts have "no
license to relax that requirement to accommodate the class device." See id. at
431 & n.6. It follows that a class of persons whose records were merely
over-retained -- undisclosed and unused -- cannot recover damages, because the
overwhelming majority of them, like the named plaintiff, suffered no concrete
harm.

     This principle independently dooms the certified class. The class is
defined as "all persons whose covered records were retained ... beyond the
period permitted by the CDPA." (App. 41.) By that definition, membership turns
on the fact of over-retention, not on any harm. The class therefore
necessarily "includes both injured and uninjured" persons, and indeed is
composed almost entirely of uninjured persons. Certifying such a class squarely
violates TransUnion's command. At a minimum, the class definition sweeps in
vast numbers of members with no standing, which -- as Part II explains -- also
means individualized standing inquiries would swamp any common question. See
Olean, 31 F.4th at 669 & n.14 (a class with "more than a de minimis number" of
uninjured members raises predominance problems). [8]

     The district court brushed this aside by reasoning that standing could be
sorted out at the damages phase. (App. 31.) But standing is not a damages
question. It is the "threshold" question of the court's power to act at all,
Summers v. Earth Island Institute, 555 U.S. 488, 492-93 (2009), and it must be
resolved before the merits, not deferred to a later stage where the class
device has already done its work. Deferring standing until after certification
inverts the constitutional order of operations and is reversible error.

II.  THE CLASS WAS IMPROPERLY CERTIFIED BECAUSE INDIVIDUAL ISSUES PREDOMINATE
     OVER COMMON ONES.

     Even setting standing aside, certification was an abuse of discretion. A
Rule 23(b)(3) class may be certified only if "questions of law or fact common
to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual
members." The predominance inquiry "tests whether proposed classes are
sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation." Amchem
Products, Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997). It is "far more demanding"
than Rule 23(a)'s commonality requirement. Id. at 623-24. The district court's
order fails that demanding test at every step.

A.   Rule 23(b)(3) predominance requires a rigorous analysis the district court
     did not perform.

     A district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" before certifying a
class, and that analysis will "frequently entail overlap with the merits of
the plaintiff's underlying claim." Comcast, 569 U.S. at 33-34 (quotation marks
omitted); accord Wal-Mart, 564 U.S. at 351. The court "must resolve" the
factual and legal disputes bearing on certification, "even if those disputes
overlap with the merits." Olean, 31 F.4th at 664-65; see also In re Hydrogen
Peroxide Antitrust Litigation, 552 F.3d 305, 320 (3d Cir. 2008). It is not
enough to identify a common question; the court must determine whether common
questions predominate, which requires "weigh[ing]" the common issues against
the individual ones. Olean, 31 F.4th at 664; 7AA Charles Alan Wright & Arthur
R. Miller, Federal Practice and Procedure Section 1778 (3d ed.).

     The district court performed no such weighing. It identified a single
common question -- "did Nordhaven over-retain records" -- and treated its
answer as dispositive of predominance. (App. 46.) But the existence of one
common question does not establish predominance; "[w]hat matters to class
certification ... is not the raising of common questions ... but, rather the
capacity of a classwide proceeding to generate common answers apt to drive the
resolution of the litigation." Wal-Mart, 564 U.S. at 350 (quotation marks
omitted; emphasis in original). The common question the court identified does
not drive resolution, because over-retention alone does not establish
liability to any particular plaintiff. What drives resolution -- for Ms.
Calloway and for every absent member -- is whether that member suffered a
concrete, compensable injury. And that question, as Part I shows, is
irreducibly individual. [9]

B.   Injury and causation turn on individualized proof.

     Once standing is taken seriously, the elements that decide who may recover
are individual, not common. For each of the 1.4 million class members, the
factfinder would have to determine: whether that member's entry was ever
disclosed; if so, to whom; whether the disclosure caused any consequence;
whether the member knew of and relied on anything; and whether the member
suffered any harm bearing a close relationship to a traditional tort. These are
precisely the "individualized" questions that predominate over the single
common question of over-retention. [10]

     TransUnion itself illustrates the problem. There, the class had to be
sorted into those whose reports were disclosed (who had standing and a harm)
and those whose reports were not (who did not). That sorting is an individual
inquiry, member by member. A class defined by over-retention alone forces the
same sorting on a far larger scale, because the class was not limited to
persons whose records were disclosed. The district court's response -- that
disclosure and reliance go only to "damages" -- misunderstands the claim. In a
suit where the injury IS disclosure-and-effect, disclosure and effect are
elements of liability, not measures of damages. Relabeling an element of
liability as a damages question does not make the individual inquiry disappear;
it merely hides it. Wal-Mart forbids exactly that maneuver, holding that a
court may not "replace" individualized proof of entitlement with a sampling or
averaging device that assumes the answer. 564 U.S. at 367 ("Trial by Formula").

     Tyson Foods does not help the plaintiff. There, the Court permitted
representative statistical proof because each class member could have used the
same evidence "to establish liability if he or she had brought an individual
action," and the sample stood in for evidence "each employee" could have
offered. Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo, 577 U.S. 442, 455-58 (2016). Here,
by contrast, no common sample can prove that a particular member's record was
disclosed and caused harm, because that fact varies from member to member and
is, for the great majority, simply absent. Tyson Foods expressly reaffirmed
that representative evidence is impermissible where it "conceal[s]" the absence
of individualized proof. Id. at 459-60. That is this case. [11]

C.   The damages model does not match the liability theory and cannot be
     measured classwide.

     Certification independently fails under Comcast because the plaintiff's
damages model does not correspond to her theory of liability. Comcast holds
that "a model purporting to serve as evidence of damages ... must measure only
those damages attributable to that theory" of liability; "[i]f the model does
not even attempt to do that, it cannot possibly establish that damages are
susceptible of measurement across the entire class." 569 U.S. at 35. Where the
model and the theory diverge, "[q]uestions of individual damage calculations
will inevitably overwhelm questions common to the class." Id.

     The plaintiff's model is to award the statutory minimum of $100 to every
class member, multiplied by the number of over-retained entries. (App. 54.) The
district court praised this as simple "arithmetic." But its very simplicity is
the defect: the model measures the number of entries, not any harm. It assigns
the same $100 to a member whose record was disclosed and misused and to a
member -- like Ms. Calloway -- whose record was never seen by anyone. A model
that awards identical damages regardless of injury is not measuring the injury;
it is measuring the violation. Under Comcast, that is not a permissible damages
model, because it does not "measure only those damages attributable to" a
theory under which harm is the gravamen. Id. [12]

     The plaintiff will respond that the CDPA authorizes statutory damages,
which need not be proved individually. But statutory damages do not dissolve
Article III or Rule 23. A statutory-damages remedy still requires a plaintiff
with a concrete injury (Part I), and a classwide statutory-damages theory still
requires that the predicate for liability be common (Part II.A-B). The CDPA's
per-consumer, per-incident structure, keyed to a culpability-graded range of
$100 to $750, presupposes an individualized assessment of each incident's
gravity -- the opposite of the flat, classwide multiplication the plaintiff
proposes. See Section III, infra. The damages model thus fails Comcast twice
over: it neither matches the liability theory nor reflects the statute's own
individualized structure. [13]

III. THE DISTRICT COURT MISCONSTRUED THE CDPA TO IMPOSE STRICT LIABILITY AND
     THAT CONSTRUCTION IS PREEMPTED.

     The certification order also rests on a legal error about the CDPA itself.
The district court read the statute to impose liability for over-retention
"regardless of intent," so that an inadvertent configuration error is as
actionable as a deliberate refusal to delete. (App. 34.) That reading is not
compelled by the text, is contrary to the statute's structure, and -- if
adopted -- conflicts with federal law. Because the error infected both the
merits ruling and the certification analysis (which assumed a strict-liability
common question), it requires reversal. [14]

A.   The statute's text requires a knowing violation.

     Statutory interpretation begins with the text, read in context. Ohio v.
American Express Co., 585 U.S. 529, 540 (2018). The CDPA's enforcement
provision creates a cause of action for "any consumer injured by a violation of
this title." Cal. Civ. Code Section 1799.31 (fictional). The word "violation,"
in the context of a statute that grades damages by severity across a range,
carries a culpability requirement. The damages provision authorizes "not less
than $100 and not more than $750 per consumer per incident." A graduated
penalty range keyed to severity makes sense only if the statute contemplates
that some violations are more culpable than others -- which in turn presupposes
that culpability is an element. A strict-liability reading renders the range's
upper reaches meaningless, because an inadvertent, promptly corrected error and
a willful refusal would be treated identically at the $100 floor the plaintiff
proposes to apply classwide. [15]

     The presumption against strict liability reinforces the text. Courts do
not lightly read a statute to impose liability without fault, particularly
where doing so would expose defendants to crushing, disproportionate penalties
for innocent conduct. The district court's construction would make every
transient technical error -- a delayed batch job, a misconfigured schedule -- a
multimillion-dollar tort, without regard to diligence, notice, or correction.
That is a result the legislature is presumed not to have intended absent clear
language, and the CDPA contains none. Reading "violation" to require at least
knowledge harmonizes the enforcement provision with the graduated-damages
provision and avoids the absurdity of strict, classwide liability for
good-faith mistakes. [16]

     The district court's contrary reading drew on the statute's use of the
mandatory "shall not retain." But the presence of a mandatory obligation does
not answer the question of the mental state required to be liable in damages
for breaching it. Many mandatory obligations are enforced only against knowing
violators. The obligation and the scienter of its enforcement are distinct
questions, and the court conflated them. [17]

B.   The district court's construction conflicts with federal law and is
     preempted.

     Finally, to the extent the CDPA is construed to impose strict, classwide
liability on a data intermediary for retaining audit fingerprints of records
the customer independently maintains under federal law, it is preempted. The
Fair Credit Reporting Act comprehensively regulates the accuracy, use, and
retention of consumer-report information, and it calibrates liability to
culpability, distinguishing negligent from willful noncompliance. 15 U.S.C.
Sections 1681n, 1681o. A state rule that imposes flat strict liability on the
custodian of derivative integrity records, untethered from any use or
disclosure, "stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the
full purposes and objectives" of that federal scheme. Geier v. American Honda
Motor Co., 529 U.S. 861, 873 (2000); Hughes v. Talen Energy Marketing, LLC,
578 U.S. 150, 162-63 (2016). [18]

     The ordinary presumption against preemption, Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator
Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947); Wyeth, 555 U.S. at 565, does not save the
district court's construction, because the conflict here is direct: federal law
deliberately ties liability to fault and to actual misuse, and the district
court's CDPA severs both ties. Where a state-law duty makes it impossible to
comply with the federal balance, or frustrates its purposes, the state duty
must yield. Wyeth, 555 U.S. at 563-64; Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr, 518 U.S. 470,
485 (1996). At a minimum, the serious preemption question counsels adopting the
knowing-violation construction under the canon of constitutional avoidance,
which independently defeats the strict-liability premise on which the class was
certified. [19]


                                CONCLUSION

     The judgment certifying the class and denying dismissal should be
reversed. The Court should hold that Ms. Calloway and the absent class members
who allege only over-retention, without disclosure or concrete harm, lack
Article III standing; that individual questions predominate and preclude
certification under Rule 23(b)(3); and that the CDPA requires a knowing
violation. The case should be remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims
of all plaintiffs who cannot establish concrete harm.
